## PART 1 — HOW POWER ACTUALLY WORKS (Academic Grounding)

### 1.1 The three faces of power (Lukes, 1974/2005)

Steven Lukes' *Power: A Radical View* is the foundational framework. Power operates on three levels:

| Face | Mechanism | What it looks like in Pierce County |
|------|-----------|-------------------------------------|
| **1st — Decision-making** | Winning in open conflict | Mello's budget, Council votes, zoning decisions |
| **2nd — Non-decision-making** | Setting the agenda; keeping issues OFF the table | What never gets discussed at Council; the Chamber's Voters Guide shaping which candidates are "viable" |
| **3rd — Ideological** | Shaping preferences so people don't even perceive their interests | The "Tacoma revival" narrative; "growth is good" consensus; the belief that business interests = community interests |

**Key insight:** The most powerful actors are often those who never need to *win* a fight because the fight never happens. Bachrach & Baratz (1962) called this the "second face" — power to create "barriers to the public airing of policy conflicts." Our map captures the 1st face well (who wins votes, who controls budgets) but is weakest on the 2nd and 3rd faces (who controls the agenda and the narrative).

### 1.2 The community power debate: elitism vs. pluralism

- **Floyd Hunter (1953)** — *Community Power Structure*: studied Atlanta, found a small business elite that governed behind the scenes via reputation and interlocking networks.
- **Robert Dahl (1961)** — *Who Governs?*: studied New Haven, found pluralism — different groups win on different issues; no single elite.
- **G. William Domhoff (1978)** — *Who Really Rules?*: re-examined New Haven, found Dahl wrong — a corporate elite did dominate.

**The realistic synthesis:** The evidence in this case leans **elitist** (Hunter/Domhoff), not pluralist. The Chamber's Candidate Academy, the interlocking boards (Wiborg, Ponepinto, Lawver), the concentration of campaign money, and the revolving door (Pierson as both Chamber CEO and Acting County Economic Development Director) all point to a cohesive business-political elite — not a pluralist marketplace of competing groups. This is a testable claim, not an assumption.

### 1.3 Mills' power elite (1956)

C. Wright Mills argued power concentrates in three interlocked institutional hierarchies: **corporate, military, and political**. Pierce County maps almost perfectly onto this:

| Mills' triad | Pierce County equivalent |
|--------------|--------------------------|
| Corporate | MultiCare, Columbia Bank, Chamber, developers, Saltchuk/Foss |
| Military | JBLM (I Corps, 7th ID, SOF) |
| Political | County Executive, Council, state delegation (Jinkins as Speaker) |

Mills' key claim: these elites share **overlapping backgrounds, social circles, and interests** that reinforce cohesion. Our interlock data (Wiborg on MultiCare/Chamber/GTCF/UW Tacoma; Ponepinto on United Way/TAM/TCC/Chamber; Lawver on GTCF/United Way/labor) is direct evidence of this cohesion.

### 1.4 Galbraith's typology (1983) — *The Anatomy of Power*

Galbraith distinguishes three instruments of power:
- **Condign** (force/punishment) — Sheriff Swank, police, JBLM, criminal networks
- **Compensatory** (rewards/resources) — MultiCare's $7.2B, the Tribe's gaming revenue, campaign money, foundation grants
- **Conditioned** (persuasion/belief) — the Chamber's narrative control, media, the "revival" story, education

**Realistic insight:** The most durable power is **conditioned** — it doesn't require force or payment because people believe the arrangement is natural. The Chamber's power is largely conditioned (it shapes what "good for business" means, and that framing wins). This is why the Chamber scored CRITICAL despite having no formal authority.

### 1.5 Regime theory & the growth machine (Stone; Logan & Molotch)

- **Logan & Molotch (1987)** — *Urban Fortunes*: cities are "growth machines" — coalitions of land-based elites (developers, realtors, banks, media) who profit from growth and dominate local politics to pursue it.
- **Clarence Stone (1989)** — *Regime Politics*: governing is a "regime" — an informal but durable partnership between government and private actors who need each other.

**Pierce County fit:** The Chamber + developers (Rush, Miles Sand & Gravel, NASH Cascadia, Southport) + realtors + banks + MultiCare + Port form a textbook **growth machine**. The Tideflats subarea plan, the manufacturing push, the housing debate (Chamber "supports housing while opposing price controls") — all are growth-machine politics. The regime is the informal partnership between this coalition and the county/city governments.

### 1.6 Bourdieu: symbolic capital & the field

Bourdieu adds that power is not just material — it's **symbolic capital** (prestige, legitimacy, recognition) that converts across fields. The Museum of Glass board, the Tacoma Art Museum, the "revival" narrative, the Chamber's Economic Index — these are symbolic capital machines. They convert economic power into cultural legitimacy, which then converts back into political influence.

### 1.7 Foucault: disciplinary power & governmentality

Foucault's contribution: power is not just held by actors but **embedded in systems, institutions, and knowledge**. The county's 30+ boards, the permitting apparatus, the zoning code, the budget process — these are disciplinary structures that shape what's possible. The most powerful position is often the one that **defines the categories** (what counts as "blight," "affordable," "viable").

## PART 2 — THE PESSIMISTIC / REALISTIC LENS

The academic frameworks above are the theory. Here's the hard-nosed, "how the world actually works" reading of them — the lens that separates a naive map from a true one.

### 2.1 Power is not what's on the org chart — it's who can say "no" and make it stick

The most powerful actors are those with **veto power** — the ability to block. In Pierce County:
- **The Puyallup Tribe** can say "no" to county jurisdiction via sovereignty — the only actor that can *opt out*.
- **JBLM** can say "no" to land use around the base (AICUZ buffers).
- **MultiCare** can say "no" to a hospital project by simply not building it.
- **The Chamber** can say "no" to a candidate by making them non-viable.
- **The Sheriff** can say "no" to the executive's authority (and has, via lawsuit).

The org chart (Mello, Council) shows who *can* act; the veto map shows who *actually* governs. **Our composite score underweights veto power** because it's network-based (centrality), not veto-based.

### 2.2 Money follows power, but power also follows information

The campaign finance data shows money flowing to candidates. But the deeper pattern is **information asymmetry**: the Chamber, the foundations, the developers, and the county staff all know more about how the system works than the public does. The Chamber's Economic Index, the county's budget documents, the permitting process — these are information advantages. **Whoever controls the information controls the agenda** (Lukes' 2nd face). Our map captures money but underweights information control.

### 2.3 The revolving door is the real pipeline

The single most damning pattern in the evidence: **Tom Pierson** was simultaneously Interim CEO of the Chamber AND Acting Director of Economic Development for Pierce County. That's not a conflict of interest — that's the same person on both sides of the table. Similarly:
- Ryan Mello: GTCF board → County Executive
- Rosie Ayala: GTCF board → Council
- JT Wilcox: State House Republican Leader → Port Commissioner
- Dona Ponepinto: United Way CEO + TAM board chair + TCC board chair + Chamber board
- Nathe Lawver: labor + GTCF + United Way

**The realistic reading:** Pierce County is governed by a small, interlocking, self-reinforcing elite that rotates through government, business, philanthropy, and labor. The formal institutions are real, but they're staffed by people who already know each other from the same boards. This is Mills' power elite, operating at county scale.

### 2.4 The "growth machine" is the default ideology

The most powerful belief in Pierce County is that **growth is good and inevitable**. The Chamber, the developers, the Port, the realtors, the banks, and much of the media all share this. It's not a conspiracy — it's a **structural incentive**: everyone in the coalition profits from growth (land values, contracts, construction, tax base). The result is that alternatives (managed decline, degrowth, heavy redistribution) are never seriously on the table. This is Lukes' 3rd face operating at full strength.

### 2.5 The enforcement vacuum is where power hides

The most important structural finding: **no one enforces the rules against the powerful.** The county's ethics board is advisory. The state doesn't enforce RCW 42.23 against council members. The Chamber's Candidate Academy alumni are secret. The Advocacy Fund donors are secret. The 911 fund diversion was caught by the state auditor, not internal controls. **Power concentrates where accountability is absent.**

### 2.6 The pessimistic bottom line

If you want the honest, unvarnished version: **Pierce County is a growth machine run by an interlocking business-political-philanthropic elite, with the formal government as the visible surface of a deeper regime.** The elected officials are real and matter, but they operate within a field shaped by actors who never face an election: the Chamber, the foundations, the developers, the healthcare systems, the Tribe, and JBLM. The map we've built captures the visible nodes; the *real* power is in the edges — the interlocks, the revolving door, the agenda control, the information asymmetry.

### 3.2 The composite power score — what it measures and what it misses

The composite score (35% weighted degree + 25% betweenness + 20% eigenvector + 20% PageRank) measures **network centrality** — how connected and how central a node is in the relationship graph. This is a legitimate, academically-grounded measure (Hunter, Wasserman & Faust, Freeman, Bonacich, Brin & Page).

**But it systematically underweights:**
1. **Veto power** — the ability to block, which the Tribe, JBLM, MultiCare, and the Sheriff have in spades but which doesn't show up as centrality.
2. **Agenda control** — the Chamber's power to shape which candidates are viable and which issues are discussed (Lukes' 2nd face).
3. **Narrative/ideological power** — the "growth is good" consensus, the "revival" story (Lukes' 3rd face, Bourdieu's symbolic capital).
4. **Information asymmetry** — who knows how the system works.
5. **Durability** — the Tribe's sovereignty and the Chamber's 141-year institutional memory are structural, not just network positions.

**The realistic correction:** The composite score is a good *network* measure but a partial *power* measure. The top-5 (Mello, Council, Tribe, MultiCare, JBLM) are defensible, but the Chamber (scored CRITICAL in the gap analysis) and the interlock nodes (Wiborg, Ponepinto, Lawver) are likely **under-ranked** because their power is agenda/narrative/veto power, not centrality.

### 4.4 The biggest structural gap

**We have mapped the nodes and edges, but not the FIELD** (Bourdieu) — the rules, incentives, and taken-for-granted assumptions that make the whole system reproduce itself. The most powerful thing in Pierce County is not any single actor but the **growth-machine ideology** that everyone — including the reformers — operates within. That's the 3rd face of power, and it's the hardest to document because it's invisible to those inside it.

5. **Fill the religious and family-dynasty gaps** with targeted research.
6. **Reframe the final narrative** around the structural finding: Pierce County is a growth machine run by an interlocking elite, with the formal government as the visible surface of a deeper regime.
