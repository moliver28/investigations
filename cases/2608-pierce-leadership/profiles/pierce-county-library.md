- **Why it's on the map:** Pierce County Library System is included as a junior taxing library district under state law (RCW) 27.12. It raises property taxes over a defined service area. ## 1. Who they are
- **Exact legal name:** Pierce County Library System (PCLS). Formally the Pierce County Rural Library District.
- **Entity type:** Washington junior taxing library district. A government entity under RCW 27.12 (Library Districts). It is separate from municipal libraries (Tacoma Public Library, Puyallup Public Library) and from the Pierce County government's general-purpose departments.
- **Formed:** Established by ballot measure passed by voters in unincorporated Pierce County on November 7, 1944. Began operating January 2, 1946. Opened seven station branches in its first year.
- **Service area:** Unincorporated Pierce County plus 15 cities and towns that have annexed for library service. Excludes the City of Tacoma (which has its own library system) and the City of Puyallup (which has its own library system).
- **Branches:** 20 library locations. They include:

Busiest: Gig Harbor, South Hill, Lakewood, University Place, Parkland / Spanaway.
- **Board of Trustees:** Five members. They are appointed by the Pierce County Executive (current executive: Ryan Mello). Five-year terms, with the option of up to two consecutive terms. Meetings are the second Wednesday of each month at 3:30 pm at the Administrative Center.
- **Executive leadership:** Executive Director Gretchen Caserotti (one of five directors in PCLS history; predecessors: Marion Cromwell, Carolyn Else, Neel Parikh, Georgia Lomax).
- **Collection:** About 1.2 million items. Circulation about 6.9 to 7.4 million items a year. 334,362 members (2016). About 2.2 million in-person visits a year (2016). Website had about 3.5 million visitors (2016).

## 3. Where their power comes from
- **Power from the law:** As a library district under RCW 27.12, PCLS can levy property taxes (subject to Washington's 1% cap, also called the levy lid). It can issue bonds with voter approval. It can sign contracts, hire staff, buy property, and provide library services inside its boundaries.
- **Taxing power:** Property taxes are the main funding source per PCLS's own statement: "Property taxes are the primary source of funding for the Library System." The 2018 levy (Levy Sustainability Fund) was approved by voters. It builds savings for long-term stability. Per the 2026 Budget Book, Washington's 1% property tax cap limits annual revenue growth. In 2025, General Fund expenses exceeded revenues. The budget had to be balanced carefully.
- **Budget control (2026 figures):** Total budget about $31.9M (2016 reference figure from Wikipedia, citing Washington Public Library Statistical Report 2016). The 2026 budget summaries show several separate funds:
 - General Fund operating budget.
 - Capital Improvement Fund.
 - Election Fund.
 - Debt Service Fund.
 - Levy Sustainability Fund.
 - Property and Facility Fund.
The specific 2026 total is UNVERIFIED — budget summary PDFs show fund-level detail but the total was not added up in this pass.
- **How it touches government:**
 - **Board picks:** The Pierce County Executive (Ryan Mello since Jan 2025) appoints all five trustees. They are subject to County Council confirmation (UNVERIFIED — confirmation rules vary by RCW).
 - **Annual budget approval:** The Board adopts the annual budget. It is subject to public hearings and final public-hearing rules per RCW 27.12.
 - **State Auditor oversight:** Subject to SAO accountability audits (UNVERIFIED — specific 2024 / 2025 SAO report not pulled).
 - **Levy lid:** Subject to Washington's 1% annual property-tax revenue growth cap (RCW 84.55). Lifting the lid requires voter approval.
- **Where the domains overlap:**
 - **gov ↔ social:** Library services to about 680,000 residents. Summer reading programs. Children's programs. Community events.
 - **gov ↔ econ:** Big spending on books, databases, technology. Capital projects for branch upkeep. Recent or upcoming: Gig Harbor closure for maintenance Sept 14 to 26; Steilacoom closure Sept 28 to Oct 13.

## 1. Who they are
- **Verdict:** PCLS runs a multi-million-dollar public library district. It delivers lending, programming, and community-space services to about 680,000 Pierce County residents across 20 branches. Its power is mostly fiscal and service-delivery. It levies property taxes (subject to the 1% cap). It adopts annual budgets. It runs a regional library system. The five-member Board of Trustees — appointed by the Pierce County Executive — sets policy direction. PCLS is limited by Washington's property-tax cap (RCW 84.55). It is also limited by voter approval rules for levy lid lifts and bonds. In 2025, General Fund expenses exceeded revenues, creating fiscal pressure. The 2018 Levy Sustainability Fund was set up to manage that constraint.
- **Conflicts of interest:** Several Board members have direct professional ties to Pierce County or its affiliated groups. Ryan Wheaton is the Chief Planning Officer at Pierce Transit (a separate Pierce County agency). Pam Duncan chairs the Tacoma-Pierce County Affordable Housing Consortium. She chairs the Washington State Community Action Partnership. She also sits on the Washington Low-Income Housing Alliance. Tono Sablan sits on the Greater Tacoma Community Foundation Board. These cross-board memberships create alignment of interests in funding and policy choices. No specific conflict has been alleged. Trustee picks by the County Executive (Ryan Mello since Jan 2025) create political accountability but no formal confirmation gate is documented — UNVERIFIED.
- **The other reading:** An opposing reading: PCLS is a service-delivery group with limited political power. Its choices affect library patrons but not the broader political economy of Pierce County. It is one of several library systems in the county (Tacoma Public Library, Puyallup Public Library). It competes for patron attention. The 2025 General Fund shortfall suggests fiscal pressure. That pressure may force service cuts or a levy lid lift (which would need voter approval). The trustee-appointment setup by the County Executive makes PCLS open to political capture. It also brings democratic accountability through executive elections. The board's makeup (multiple members with nonprofit and social-service backgrounds rather than business or government) tilts its policy stance toward equity, literacy, and community services.
