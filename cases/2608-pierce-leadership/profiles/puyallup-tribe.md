## 0. Why this person or group is on the map
- **Why the Tribe is here:** The Puyallup Tribe of Indians is a federally-recognized sovereign nation. Its reservation overlaps with the City of Tacoma and unincorporated Pierce County. The Tribe's power comes from federal sovereignty, the right to put land into federal trust, and tribal courts. Its decision-making power comes from the Land Use Consultation Process, or LUCP. That process was set by the 1990 Settlement. It requires local governments to consult with the Tribe on big land-use decisions. The Tribe also donates to both major parties. It lobbies the County Council. It is a top-10 employer in Pierce County.
- **Who is left out:** The Nisqually Indian Tribe is a separate sovereign. It sits on the Thurston and Pierce border. It is outside the main Pierce County boundary. The Muckleshoot Tribe is in Auburn, King County. It is excluded too. The Tribe's business arms — Tahoma Global Logistics, Marine View Ventures, and Ames International — are companies, not sovereign-power nodes. They are excluded from this profile.

## 1. Who they are
- **Exact legal name:** Puyallup Tribe of Indians (spuyaləpabš).
- **Entity type:** Federally-recognized Indian Tribe. Established 1936 under the Indian Reorganization Act. Reservation established 1854 via the Treaty of Medicine Creek.
- **Governing law:** Tribal constitution (1936). Federal Indian law (Title 25 of the United States Code, or 25 USC). The Puyallup Tribe of Indians Settlement Act of 1989, Public Law 101-41, 103 Stat. 83. The Land Use Consultation Process, or LUCP, under the 1990 Settlement Agreement.
- **Sovereignty status:** Federal sovereignty. The Tribe has a government-to-government relationship with the U.S., the State of Washington, and local governments. Tribal courts include the Puyallup Tribal Court, the Court of Appeals, and the Children's Court. They are co-equal with the Tribal Council.
- **Enrolled members:** About 4,000, per Wikipedia and the Northwest Portland Area Indian Health Board. About 2,500 live on the reservation. Reservation total population is about 41,000, mostly non-tribal.
- **Reservation:** Established 1854 by the Treaty of Medicine Creek. Expanded in 1856. Tribal government was set up in 1936. The reservation overlaps with the City of Tacoma, the City of Fife, the City of Puyallup, and unincorporated Pierce County.

## 3. Where their power comes from
- **Federal sovereignty:** As a federally-recognized tribe, the Puyallup Tribe has a government-to-government relationship with the U.S. and Washington State. It cannot be taxed by state or local governments on tribal trust land. It has its own courts, law enforcement, and power to make rules. This is the Tribe's founding power. It operates as a separate sovereign inside Pierce County's borders.
- **Land-into-trust authority:** The 1990 Land Claims Settlement and later federal laws let the Tribe put fee lands into federal trust. One such law is S.382 of the 118th Congress — the Puyallup Tribe of Indians Land Into Trust Confirmation Act of 2023. Taking land into trust removes it from local tax rolls and zoning power. This acts like a veto over local land use. Once land goes into trust, local governments lose rule-making power over it.
- **Land Use Consultation Process (LUCP):** Under Section VIII(C) of the 1990 Settlement Agreement, when the Tribe or a local government looks at a big land-use decision inside the reservation, they must consult. This gives the Tribe a formal seat at the table. No other non-governmental group in the county has this power.
- **Gaming revenue:** Emerald Queen Casino is the Tribe's main money source. The Tribe does not charge income or property taxes. Most revenue comes from government-owned businesses: EQC, Marine View Ventures, Tahoma Global Logistics. This keeps the Tribe financially independent from the county tax base.
- **Campaign donations:** The Tribe donates to both Democratic and Republican candidates and parties. This bipartisan giving keeps access open no matter which party runs the county.
- **Lobbying:** The Tribe is a registered lobbying client with the Washington State Public Disclosure Commission (PDC). In 2021 it had 3 registered lobbyists: Ehren Flygare at $91,000, Sound Government Solutions at $29,500, and Dawn P. Vyvyan at $75,000. ## 4. Their money
- **Revenue model:** The Tribe does not collect income or property taxes. Most revenue comes from government-owned businesses: Emerald Queen Casino (main source), Marine View Ventures, Tahoma Global Logistics (launched 2023), and Ames International (a candy company, acquired). Exact revenue figures are not public. Tribal sovereignty exempts the Tribe from Internal Revenue Service (IRS) Form 990 filing.
- **Gaming revenue:** Emerald Queen Casino is the primary revenue engine. The Tribe runs two EQC locations. Charity Trust Board donations come from EQC revenue. They are part of an agreement with the State of Washington. That agreement covers smoking cessation, problem gambling programs, and impact costs to local governments.
- **Charitable donations:** Since 2012 the Tribe has donated more than $18 million to hundreds of local groups through its Charity Trust Board. In 2022: more than $2 million to 100+ groups. In 2024: more than $850,000 to 60 groups. These donations are a big source of soft power and community goodwill.
- **Campaign donations:** The Tribe gives to both Pierce Dems and Pierce GOP, plus individual candidates (Mello, Cruver, Hitchen, Ayala). It also donated $50,000 to the "Harry Truman Fund," a state-level committee, per TransparencyUSA.
- **Lobbying expenditures:** 2021 PDC records show 3 registered lobbyists with combined pay of about $195,545 (Flygare $91,000, Sound Government Solutions $29,500, Vyvyan $75,000). UNVERIFIED: graph claim of "$1.4 million self-reported" as largest county lobbyist — not confirmed from primary source.
- **Federal settlement:** The 1990 Land Claims Settlement provided about $77 million in cash and assets (state, local, and federal money). It also gave about 900 acres of land and a $22 million permanent trust fund for health, social, and welfare services.

## 5. How they touch government
- **Sovereignty and federal ties:** Government-to-government relationship with the U.S. (BIA, IHS), the State of Washington (Governor's Office of Indian Affairs, now cabinet-level), and local governments (Pierce County, Tacoma, Fife, Puyallup).
- **Land Use Consultation Process (LUCP):** Under the 1990 Settlement, the Tribe and local governments must consult on big land-use decisions inside the reservation. This is a formal, binding consultation process — not just advisory.
- **Land-into-trust:** S.382 of the 118th Congress (2023), the Puyallup Tribe of Indians Land Into Trust Confirmation Act, would put about 17.264 acres of Tribe-owned fee land in Pierce County into federal trust. This removes the land from local tax rolls and zoning power.
- **Lobbying (County Council):** The Tribe is the highest-weighted lobbying relationship on the Pierce County Council (weight 5 in the graph). Likely issues include land use, trust land applications, impact payments, and public safety coordination.
- **Lobbying (State):** The Tribe is a registered lobbying client with the Washington PDC. 2025 legislative wins: sports betting on in-state college games, signed by Governor Ferguson; cabinet-level elevation of the Governor's Office of Indian Affairs.
- **Campaign donations:** The Tribe gives to both parties. It donates to Pierce Dems, Pierce GOP, and individual candidates from both parties. Its 2024 voter guide endorses specific candidates.
- **SSMCP:** The Tribe sits on the Steering Committee of the South Sound Military & Communities Partnership. SSMCP coordinates Joint Base Lewis-McChord (JBLM) issues.
- **Environmental advocacy:** The Tribe opposed Tacoma LNG and Electron Hydro. It declared a climate emergency in December 2019. It is active in Puyallup River protection.

## 1. Who they are
- **Verdict:** The Puyallup Tribe of Indians holds power through four channels. (1) **Federal sovereignty** gives the Tribe a government-to-government relationship. It cannot be taxed or regulated by state or local governments on trust land. It has its own courts and law enforcement. (2) **Land-into-trust authority** lets the Tribe take land off local tax rolls and zoning. It acts like a veto over local land-use control. (3) **Gaming revenue** from EQC gives the Tribe financial independence from the county tax base. It funds an $18 million-plus charitable donation program. (4) **Bipartisan political donations and lobbying** — the Tribe donates to both parties. It is the highest-weighted lobbying relationship on the County Council. Chairman Sterud's 46 years on the council make him the longest-serving power figure in the county. Tim Reynon's role as director of the Governor's Office of Indian Affairs gives a direct line to the state's chief executive.
- **Conflicts-of-interest flags (defense-attorney counter-argument):**
  - *Donation-to-lobbying pipeline:* The Tribe donates to individual Council members (Ayala, Hitchen, Cruver). At the same time, it lobbies the Council (weight 5). A defense attorney would argue this creates an appearance of buying access on land-use and trust-land decisions.
  - *Sovereignty shield:* The Tribe's exemption from local taxes and zoning on trust land creates an imbalance. The Tribe can influence local government through donations and lobbying. But local government cannot regulate the Tribe's trust land. This is a structural imbalance.
  - *LUCP as veto:* The Land Use Consultation Process gives the Tribe a formal role in local land-use decisions. No other private group has this. A defense attorney might call this an undue veto over local democratic processes.
  - *Strobel board overlap:* Andrew Strobel's move from Tribe planning to Mello advisor creates a possible channel for tribal influence into the Executive's office.
- **Other reading:** The opposing reading is that the Tribe's power is overstated. Its roughly 4,000 enrolled members are a small share of Pierce County's roughly 920,000 people. EQC revenue, while big, is a fraction of the county's $3.5 billion budget. The LUCP is a consultation process, not a veto. Local governments can move forward with land-use decisions after consultation. The bipartisan donation strategy ($3,000-weight edges to each party) is modest compared to the Chamber's structural influence. The Tribe's sovereignty is real but limited to trust land, which is a small share of the county's land area. Under this reading, the Tribe's rank of 4 out of 163 reflects the novelty of tribal sovereignty in a power map, not the actual size of its influence on county-wide decisions.
