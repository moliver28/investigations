## 0. Why this person or group is on the map
- **Why Metro Parks is on the map:** Metro Parks Tacoma is the independent park district that runs Pierce County's largest urban park system. It covers 2,907 acres across 75 parks and facilities. It is on the map for four reasons:
  1. **From holding office:** it is a junior taxing district (a local government that can levy property taxes) with the legal right to raise property taxes for parks.
  2. **Decision-making:** its five elected Park Commissioners set policy, budget, and priorities for a $213 million two-year budget.
  3. **Reputational:** it runs nationally known places like Point Defiance Zoo & Aquarium, Northwest Trek Wildlife Park, and the 760-acre Point Defiance Park.
  4. **Well-connected:** its graph edges link to Ryan Mello (former commissioner), Anders Ibsen (Tacoma mayor), and Matt Mauer (board president).
- **Other bodies nearby:** The five-member Board of Park Commissioners is part of this profile, not a separate node. The Tacoma City Council is separate. Tacoma Public Schools is separate. Pierce County Parks and Recreation is a separate county-level agency.

## 1. Who they are
- **Verdict:** Metro Parks Tacoma runs the largest urban park system in Pierce County — 2,907 acres across 75 parks and facilities, including nationally known places like Point Defiance Zoo & Aquarium and Northwest Trek Wildlife Park. Its power comes from its legal taxing authority (junior taxing district with a property-tax levy capped at $0.60 per $1,000 of assessed value) and from democratic governance (five elected commissioners on six-year terms). The 2025-2026 biennial operating budget is about $213 million. The capital plan is about $25 million. The Park District is in a budget crisis — about a $17 million deficit in 2025-2026, around 21% budget cuts, a $4 million deficit in 2025, and a projected $3 million in 2026. That shows its taxing authority has not kept up with operating costs, forcing major cuts and a search for new revenue (fees, grants, voter-approved levies).
- **Conflicts-of-interest flags:** As an independent junior-taxing district, Metro Parks can raise property taxes without direct City Council approval. The six-year board terms insulate commissioners from short-term voter pressure. The 2025 board turnover (3 of 5 seats vacated in 4 months — Ayala to Council, Pointer retirement, Reid death) shows how fragile long-tenured governance can be. The current budget crisis pits keeping park services against raising taxes and fees. Counter: an elected board gives democratic accountability. The crisis reflects real cost pressures (inflation, post-COVID recovery), not mismanagement. The Park District's tribal-land acknowledgement (Coast Salish) and its focus on equity in Alisa Lee's appointment show awareness of representational gaps.
- **Analysis of alternatives:** Opposing reading: Metro Parks' power is overstated. Its junior-taxing-district status is limited by the $0.60 per $1,000 levy cap, and the 2025-2026 budget crisis shows the model is at its limit. Under this view, Metro Parks is over-extended and could face service cuts, governance reform, or even consolidation. But Metro Parks' land assets (2,907 acres) are durable and irreplaceable. The Park District has survived 119 years. The 2025 board turnover may bring new energy to revenue and governance questions. The bottom line: Metro Parks holds real power through land and institutional assets, but its operating budget is under structural pressure.
