## 0. Why this person or group is on the map
- **Why we added Sound Transit:** Sound Transit is a regional government agency. Its full name is the Central Puget Sound Regional Transit Authority. It plans, builds, and runs high-capacity transit across King, Pierce, and Snohomish Counties. It has a multi-billion-dollar budget. Voters have approved its taxing power. Its 18-member Board includes Pierce County Executive Ryan Mello as Vice Chair. All this makes it a key power node for roads and money in Pierce County. The agency faces a $34.5 billion ST3 budget shortfall. That pressure shapes regional transportation decisions for decades to come.
- **Other agencies we did not add:** Pierce Transit is mapped separately as `pierce-transit`. It is a separate local agency. King County Metro is outside Pierce County. WSDOT, the Washington State Department of Transportation, is a state agency mapped separately if applicable. PSRC, the Puget Sound Regional Council, is a planning body, not a transit operator.

## 1. Who they are
- **Legal name:** Central Puget Sound Regional Transit Authority (doing business as Sound Transit).
- **Entity type:** A government regional transit authority. It is a municipal corporation formed under state law. The laws are RCW 81.104 (Public Transportation Systems) and RCW 81.112 (Regional Transit Authorities).
- **Governing law:** RCW 81.104 and RCW 81.112. The King, Pierce, and Snohomish County councils created the agency under RCW 81.112.030.
- **Voter approvals:** ST1 (November 5, 1996), ST2 (November 4, 2008), and ST3 (November 8, 2016). Voters in the Sound Transit district approved each one to fund regional high-capacity transit.
- **Board structure:** 18-member Board of Directors. Members include elected officials from the three member counties and the WSDOT Secretary. As of February 2026: Dave Somers (Chair, Snohomish County Executive), Claudia Balducci (Vice Chair, King County Council member), and Ryan Mello (Vice Chair, Pierce County Executive).
- **Executive leadership:** CEO Dow Constantine (former King County Executive). The Board appointed him on March 27, 2025. He officially joined in April 2025. Interim CEO Goran Sparrman came before him.

## 3. Where their power comes from
- **Statutory authority:** Authorized under RCW 81.104 and RCW 81.112. It can plan, build, and permanently run high-capacity transit for the central Puget Sound region. It can levy voter-approved sales taxes, property taxes, and motor vehicle excise taxes, or MVET. It can also issue bonds.
- **Budget scale:** The 2026 adopted budget has $3.3 billion in revenues and other financing sources. It has $3.5 billion in expenses and outlays. Total revenues dropped 26.1% ($1.2 billion) from 2025. That drop comes mainly from lower bond and TIFIA loan proceeds. TIFIA stands for Transportation Infrastructure Finance and Innovation Act. It is a federal loan program. Tax revenues make up 75% of total revenue. The 2026 property tax revenue is $183 million (up 3.8% from $176.2 million in 2025). The Board approved a 1% property tax increase to get there. Passenger fare revenue is $85 million (28.4% higher than the 2025 forecast).
- **ST3 expansion program:** Sound Transit 3 (approved November 2016) is a $54 billion or more capital program. It faces a $34.5 billion budget shortfall because construction costs have soared. The Board adopted Motion M2025-36. That motion tells staff to build a new Regional Transit Long-Range Plan. It is the first major update since 2014. The Board aims to adopt it in the third quarter of 2026. That plan will set the framework for ST4.
- **Domain overlap:**
 - **Government meets economy:** The multi-billion-dollar capital program creates construction jobs, contracts, and transit-oriented development. The Equitable Transit-Oriented Development (TOD) program includes 234 affordable homes at the Spring District in Bellevue.
 - **Government meets institutions:** The Board is made up of elected officials from member counties and cities. The WSDOT Secretary is an ex officio member. Ex officio means the seat is automatic because of the office held.
 - **Government meets politics:** Board members are elected officials. Their work on Sound Transit touches their own electoral politics.

## 5. How they touch government
- **Taxing power:** Sound Transit levies voter-approved sales taxes, property taxes, and MVET inside the Sound Transit district. 2026 property tax revenue is $183 million. The Board approved a 1% property tax increase for 2026.
- **Budget adoption:** The 2026 Adopted Budget and Financial Plan ($3.3 billion revenue / $3.5 billion expenses). The system expansion budget is $1.9 billion (mostly Stride bus rapid transit, or BRT, at $710 million). Debt service is $230 million. Operations and maintenance outlays are $38 million annualized. The estimated cash position at the end of 2025 is about $7.2 billion.
- **Bond issuance:** Sound Transit holds AA and AAA credit ratings. It issues bonds and uses TIFIA loans for capital financing. Lower bond and TIFIA proceeds drove the 26.1% revenue drop from 2025.
- **Pierce County representation:** Ryan Mello (Pierce County Executive) serves as Vice Chair. He also sits on the System Expansion Committee. Kristina Walker (Tacoma City Council member) sits on the Board. She chairs the Rider Experience and Operations Committee. Hunter George (Fircrest Council member) also sits on the Board.
- **Federal grants:** Sound Transit receives Federal Transit Administration (FTA) formula grants (sections 5307, 5337, and 5339). It also receives Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) Surface Transportation Block Grant (STBG) and Congestion Mitigation and Air Quality (CMAQ) funds through PSRC project selection.
- **State funding:** Move Ahead Washington is a state transportation package. It was led by Rep. Jake Fey (D-27). It includes state transportation funds that flow to Sound Transit projects.
- **Legislative ties:** The Washington State Legislature appropriates transportation funding. Sound Transit works with WSDOT. WSDOT Secretary Julie Meredith sits on the Board.

## 1. Who they are
- **Verdict:** Sound Transit's power comes from three things. First, its legal standing as a regional transit authority under RCW 81.104 and 81.112. Second, its voter-approved taxes on sales, property, and vehicles. Third, its huge capital program (ST3 is over $54 billion, though it now faces a $34.5 billion shortfall). The 2026 budget of $3.3 billion in revenue and $3.5 billion in spending makes it one of the largest public agencies in the Pacific Northwest. Its 18-member Board is filled with elected officials from King, Pierce, and Snohomish Counties. That creates direct board ties to county and city governments. Pierce County Executive Ryan Mello's Vice Chair seat gives Pierce County strong influence over regional transit decisions worth billions. The CEO appointment of Dow Constantine, former King County Executive and longtime Board member, centralizes executive power in a leader with deep political ties.
- **Conflicts of interest:** The $34.5 billion ST3 shortfall creates pressure. The Board must scale back voter-approved projects, find new money (a possible ST4 ballot measure), or raise taxes. Each choice creates political winners and losers. Board members are elected officials who may face voters over tax increases or service cuts. The 1% property tax increase for 2026 was approved by the Board. That Board includes officials who set county-level property tax policy. CEO Constantine's move from King County Executive (a Board seat) to agency CEO creates a revolving-door concern. The Equitable TOD program (234 affordable homes at the Spring District) channels public funds to private developers.
- **The other view:** Sound Transit's power is limited by voter approval rules. It cannot raise taxes without a ballot measure. ST1, ST2, and ST3 were all voter-approved. The $34.5 billion shortfall actually weakens the agency. It cannot deliver the promised projects without more money. The Board's structure (18 elected officials with different priorities) creates internal friction. The agency's reliance on sales tax (which falls in a recession) and property tax (which is politically sensitive) limits its fiscal flexibility. The CEO pick of a politician (Constantine) rather than a transit professional could be seen as valuing political management over operational skill.
- **Confidence:** High — based on many primary sources (the Sound Transit 2026 Adopted Budget PDF, the official Board committees page, the Q2 2026 financial report, and agency resolutions) and several secondary sources (Lynnwood Times, The Center Square, The Daily UW). The budget figures, board make-up, governance structure, and the ST3 shortfall are all confirmed.
