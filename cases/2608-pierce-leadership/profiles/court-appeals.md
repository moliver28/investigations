## 0. Why this person or group is on the map
- **Why included:** decision-making + well-connected + holds office. Division II of the Washington Court of Appeals is on the map because (a) it is the appeals court of last resort for most civil and criminal cases that start in Pierce County Superior Court (a court that handles major felonies, civil suits, and family cases); (b) Pierce County is one of the 13 counties it covers; (c) its rulings bind Pierce County Superior Court judges under stare decisis (the rule that courts must follow prior decisions); (d) it sits one step above Pierce County Superior Court in the Washington court system. (Sources 1, 2, 3, 4)
- **Who was left out and why:** The Washington Supreme Court is state-level and has its own profile. Division I (Seattle) covers King, Snohomish, Skagit, Island, San Juan, and Whatcom counties. Division III is in Spokane. Pierce County Superior Court has its own profile. The Federal District Court for Western Washington has its own profile and only handles federal cases. The U.S. Ninth Circuit appeals court handles federal appeals. None of these are Division II.

## 1. Who they are
- **Full legal name:** Washington Court of Appeals, Division II.
- **Type:** State appeals court (intermediate). It is part of the unified Washington State Court System under Article IV of the Washington State Constitution.
- **Governing law:** Washington Constitution Article IV (Judiciary); Washington Court of Appeals set up by constitutional amendment; state law RCW (Revised Code of Washington) 2.06.
- **Headquarters:** 950 Broadway Plaza, Tacoma, WA 98402-4454. (Source 3)
- **Phone:** (253) 593-2970. (Source 3)
- **Parent:** Washington State Court System. The state Office of the Administrator for the Courts is the central office.
- **Subsidiaries:** None. Division II is a single court with rotating 3-judge panels.
- **Court roster (from the current WA Courts Directory):**
 - **Judges (7):** Chief Judge Bernard F. Veljacic; Acting Chief Judge Erik D. Price; Judge Meng Li Che; Judge Anne Cruser; Judge Rebecca Glasgow; Judge Linda Lee; Judge Bradley A. Maxa.
 - **Commissioners (2):** Aurora R. Bearse; Karl Triebel.
 - (Source 4)
- **Constitutional basis:** Article IV § 30 of the Washington State Constitution lets the Legislature create intermediate appeals courts. Division II was created by statute in 1969-70 and seated in 1970.

## 3. Where their power comes from
- **Power held:**
 - **Mandatory appellate jurisdiction:** Per WA Courts, "It is a non-discretionary appellate court — it must accept all appeals filed with it." (Source 2)
 - **Disposition authority:** "The Court of Appeals has authority to reverse (overrule), remand (send back to the lower court), modify, or affirm the decision of the lower court." (Source 2)
 - **Binding precedent on Pierce County:** Published opinions by Division II bind Pierce County Superior Court judges and Pierce County lower tribunals. Pierce County is in Division II's territory.
 - **No en banc review available.** (Source 1)
- **Counties covered (Division II, 13 counties):** Pierce, Thurston, Mason, Kitsap, Jefferson, Grays Harbor, Clallam, Clark, Cowlitz, Lewis, Pacific, Skamania, and Wahkiakum. (Source 2)
- **Selection / retention:** Nonpartisan election. Judges serve 6-year staggered terms. (Sources 1, 2)
- **Panel structure:** "Panels of three judges hear each appeal," per the Washington Court of Appeals. (Source 1)
- **Caseload discretion:** None. The court must accept all properly filed appeals. (Source 2)
- **Key facts:**
 - **Tax status:** Government entity (not 501(c)(3), a federal tax-exempt charity).
 - **Roster (per WA Courts Directory):** Chief Judge Bernard F. Veljacic; Acting Chief Judge Erik D. Price; Judge Meng Li Che; Judge Anne Cruser; Judge Rebecca Glasgow; Judge Linda Lee; Judge Bradley A. Maxa. Commissioners: Aurora R. Bearse; Karl Triebel. (Source 4)
 - **Government funding:** State-funded through the state Office of the Administrator for the Courts.
 - **Caseload volume:** UNVERIFIED exact figure for FY2024-25. The statewide Court of Appeals caseload is in the low thousands per year.
 - **Discretion vs. obligation:** Mandatory jurisdiction — no certiorari screening (certiorari is the discretion to pick which appeals to hear, like the U.S. Supreme Court does).

## 1. Who they are
- **Verdict:** Washington Court of Appeals Division II holds tier-3 legal power as the mandatory appeals court for Pierce County (and 12 other Washington counties). Its published opinions bind Pierce County Superior Court and Pierce County lower courts on state-law questions. FACT: It must accept all properly filed appeals (non-discretionary). FACT: It is based in Tacoma (950 Broadway Plaza) and has 7 judges + 2 commissioners per the current WA Courts Directory. FACT: It can reverse, remand, modify, or affirm lower court rulings. FACT: Judges are elected in nonpartisan elections to 6-year staggered terms. FACT: 3-judge panels hear each appeal — no en banc review. REASONING: Division II's tier-3 composite power (0.006) reflects a regional appeals court whose individual rulings bind Pierce County practice. But its weight statewide is moderate (7 of 22 judges). Its judges do not typically sit on cross-domain community boards. Its main power is doctrinal (shaping the law through written opinions). Pierce County is its largest single county, so the Pierce County bar has outsized influence on its docket.
- **Conflicts-of-interest flags (counter-argument):** A defense would say Division II is a constitutionally independent court. Its judges are publicly elected on nonpartisan ballots. Decisions are publicly published. The recusal process is set by statute. Panels are randomly assigned (which limits any single judge's ability to set the agenda). Mandatory jurisdiction means the court cannot pick and choose which cases to hear.
- **Analysis of alternatives:** The opposing view is that Division II's power is limited by (a) mandatory jurisdiction (no certiorari screening); (b) random panel assignment; (c) 3-judge panels that require a supermajority for any single ruling; (d) discretionary Supreme Court review that can override its rulings; and (e) lack of enforcement power (rulings bind but must be sent back to Superior Court for execution). Its power is doctrinal, not financial or coercive. It shapes legal practice through precedent, not budgets or hiring. The 0.006 composite score reflects a regional appeals court with binding reach over a set territory, bounded by higher review.
