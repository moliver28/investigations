## 0. Why this person or group is on the map - The Tacoma City Council is on the map for two reasons: it holds office, and it makes big decisions. It governs Tacoma (about 220,000 residents), the biggest city in the county and the county's economic core. - The council writes city laws, adopts a two-year budget of about $4.7 billion, and confirms the mayor's top appointments. Tacoma uses a council-manager system. The council sets policy. The city manager runs the city. - The council sits on screens at places like the Pierce Transit Board, Sound Transit Board, and the Tacoma-Pierce County Board of Health. This makes it a key power node. - Not on the map: the city manager (executes policy, appointed by council). The city attorney (gives legal advice). Individual council members get their own pages where they matter (Anders Ibsen, Kristina Walker). Advisory boards and ethics boards do not make decisions.

## 3. Where their power comes from - **Statutory authority:** The council writes city laws, adopts the two-year budget, confirms mayoral picks (city manager, police chief, department heads), and controls land-use rules (zoning, permits, subarea plans). The mayor can veto council laws. Six of nine council members can override a veto. - **Budget control:** The 2025–2026 two-year budget is $4.7 billion total. The General Fund faces a projected $40M structural deficit. Priority-based budget is $2B of $4.7B. The council approves the budget the mayor proposes. - **Appointment powers:** The council confirms mayoral picks for city manager, police chief, fire chief, and department director jobs. The mayor appoints committee members and chairs, subject to council approval. - **Jurisdiction:** City of Tacoma (about 220,000 residents, about 62 square miles). Tacoma is the county's largest city. Its policy choices on land use, economic development, and public safety affect the whole region. - **Domain overlaps:** Government (law-making, budget), Economic (economic development policy, business rules), Political (elected officials, campaign networks), Institutional (board seats on regional bodies).

## 1. Who they are - **Verdict:** The Tacoma City Council controls a $4.7B two-year budget and makes laws for the county's biggest city. The council's power works through the full body passing laws, adopting the budget, and confirming mayoral picks. In the council-manager system, the council sets direction while the manager runs it — so the council's power is agenda-setting and oversight, not administration. Council members' seats on regional boards (Pierce Transit, Sound Transit, Board of Health) extend the council's reach beyond city limits. The $40M General Fund deficit limits new spending. - **Conflicts-of-interest flags (defense counter-argument anticipated):** A defense attorney would point to past Open Public Meetings Act problems (Miller v. City of Tacoma 1999, 2010 controversy) — a pattern of closed-door decisions that hurt public scrutiny. The council's partnership with the Chamber of Commerce (weight 3) could create a pro-business policy tilt. Three council members on the Pierce Transit Board could create conflicts between city interests and county-wide transit priorities. The counter: the council-manager system was set up in 1952 to replace a corrupt commissioner system. Public meeting transparency has improved since the Miller ruling. - **Analysis of alternatives:** The opposing view is that the council's power is split across 9 members with no one person in charge. That makes it slow and consensus-driven, with limited ability to act fast. The mayor's veto (overridden by 6/9) is a weak tool. The $40M deficit caps new spending. Under this reading, rank 20/163 is fair, but the council's power is institutional rather than personal — no single council member wields the kind of power a county executive or prosecuting attorney does. - **Confidence:** High — council structure, current roster, budget, committee system, and government type are well-documented in primary sources (City of Tacoma website).
