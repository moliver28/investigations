Who Holds Power in Pierce County

163 people and organizations, mapped and adversarially fact-checked: an elected executive with a $3.5 billion budget, six economic anchors built on government-granted advantages, and a small set of people who sit on both sides of the table.

Research window: August 17 – September 20, 2026 · Method: public-records mapping (WA PDC, IRS 990s, .gov filings, court and corporate registries) followed by a hostile red-team review in which every claim was treated as wrong until a primary source confirmed it. ~80% of core factual claims survived. Only surviving claims appear here.

Before you read: three plain-language rules

The county has an elected Executive who runs the government, a seven-member Council that controls the budget and land use, and separately elected Sheriff and Prosecuting Attorney who answer only to voters. The Port of Tacoma is its own government with five countywide-elected commissioners. The Puyallup Tribe is a sovereign nation the county cannot tax on trust land.

Dollar figures come from public filings, and the basis is stated next to each one — a regional multiplier is labeled as regional, a settlement is labeled as a settlement, not as current wealth.

Overlaps are rendered as overlaps. Where someone donates to a candidate, sits on a board, or holds two jobs, the report states the documented fact. It does not assert a motive. One reviewer-grade tag is used throughout: verified means a primary source survived adversarial review; corrected marks the reviewed form of a claim that originally shipped wrong.

Core finding

Interpretation — labeled as such

Pierce County’s major economic institutions derive substantial power from government-granted privileges — federal appropriations, tax exemption, tribal sovereignty, land grants, and public levies — and these institutions in turn influence the government that grants those privileges. This creates a structural interdependence that warrants scrutiny.

That sentence is the thesis in the only form it survived hostile review. The sharper versions considered earlier — that the county’s private sector is “a government-created rentier class,” or that a “growth machine” is a coordinated operation — were rejected in red-team review as editorializing beyond the evidence. What the records do show is the direction of the flows below, entity by entity, with sources.

The loop as a frame, not a finding: each arrow above is documented for the six institutions below; the loop as a system is the investigation’s interpretation.

Findings

Five findings, strongest first

1. One office controls the county’s money — and holds a veto over its legislature.

The Executive proposes and signs the $3.5B biennial budget and can veto Council legislation; the Council appropriates it. Roughly 77% of the General Fund goes to law enforcement and the courts, so the elected Sheriff ($406M) and Prosecuting Attorney ($94.1M) sit at the center of county spending even as they answer to voters, not the Executive. County power concentrates in offices that cannot easily be overridden. [E001, E004]

2. All six major economic anchors rest on government-granted advantages.

Federal appropriations fund JBLM outright; tax exemption (plus Medicare/Medicaid reimbursement) underlies MultiCare’s $7.2B; federal recognition and a state gaming compact underlie the Tribe’s casino economy; Weyerhaeuser’s acreage traces to an 1864 land grant sold once, cheaply, to a railroad; the Port is a public taxing entity; the Chamber is tax-exempt. This is the finding that survived every hostile check, entity by entity, with primary documents. [E007, E009]

3. The same few people sit on both sides of the table.

Three documented dual roles anchor the interlock pattern: the Chamber’s CEO, Tom Pierson, was also appointed the county’s acting economic-development director; MultiCare’s board chair, John Wiborg, sits on the Chamber board and is a former GTCF board member; labor’s Nathe Lawver chairs United Way of Pierce County while sitting on both the GTCF and Economic Development Board. Ryan Mello himself is a former GTCF board member now holding the executive’s office. These are documented overlaps — the records show who shares tables, not what happens at them. [E008, E010]

4. Party money is lopsided; tribal money is bipartisan.

In the 2024 executive race, both finalists raised over half a million dollars ($509,801 for Mello; $549,574 for Chambers). The Republican money was concentrated — one $175,500 check from the county party plus $28,500-class transfers from caucus committees; the Democratic money was broader — unions (UFCW, IBEW 483, IAFF 726, WEA PAC), the HDCC ($28,500), and individual donors. The Puyallup Tribe gave to both parties ($2,400 to Mello among others) — documented donations from a sovereign actor with land-use business before the same governments. The report asserts the flows, not any exchange. [E004]

5. The county’s only daily newspaper is owned by an out-of-state hedge fund.

The News Tribune’s parent, McClatchy, is owned by Chatham Asset Management (NJ) — acquired in bankruptcy in 2020 through a ~$312M credit bid plus $49M cash. Its editor, Stephanie Pedersen, edits four McClatchy papers at once; the AP wire was dropped in 2024; the county has no locally owned TV station. Narrative capacity is real but extracted: what gets covered locally is decided two time zones away. [E006]

Narrative power

One daily paper, owned elsewhere

Ownership chain of the only daily: Chatham Asset Management (NJ) → McClatchy Media Company → The News Tribune (Tacoma; founded 1880; locally owned by the Baker family for 73 years until McClatchy bought it in 1986 for ~$112M). Around it: three radio groups, one business weekly, a weekly paper — and zero locally owned TV. The editor edits four papers at once; the opinion editor was appointed in 2025. In a county where the Port, the Tribe, MultiCare, and the Council all generate daily coverage decisions, the outlet that frames them is not locally owned. [E006]

What this map cannot say

Limits, open to the reader

  • The computed power score is a network measure, not a power measure. Graph centrality over-counts connected hubs (parties, the Council, courts) and under-counts isolated veto-holders (the Tribe, JBLM, the Sheriff). That is why this report uses the qualitative matrix and tier bands instead of raw scores.
  • “Shadow political party” is a characterization, not a finding. The Chamber’s Candidate Academy, Voters Guide, and Candidates Forum are documented; it officially does not endorse candidates. This report describes the programs and stops there.
  • The “growth machine” is an academic lens, not a proven organization. Donor flows between developers, realtors, banks, and the Chamber are documented; coordinated direction is not.
  • Two numbers failed verification and appear nowhere above: the MultiCare-linked “$357K against Democrats in 2022” figure (actually attributed to the hospital association’s PAC, unconfirmed against PDC C4 reports) and the claim that the Tribe is the county’s largest lobbying spender (federal lobbying of $270K in 2024 is verified; the superlative is not).
  • Corrections carried into this report: JBLM’s $12.1B is a two-county multiplier; the Tribe’s $162M is a 1990 settlement, not current assets; the tribal chairmanship rotates annually; the council appropriates to the sheriff, courts, and prosecutor (edge directions were reversed in an earlier draft); Kastama sits on Puyallup’s city council, not the county council; T’wina Nobles sits on the University Place school board.
  • Not yet checked: current council committee chairs for the 2026 session; Swank’s early biography details; a Pierce-only authoritative MultiCare headcount.

Methodology

How this was built, and verified

Researchers mapped 163 entities across six domains — government (53), institutional (47), economic (32), political (17), media (8), legal (6) — and recorded 342 relationships, each with a typed label (board seats, funding, donations, lobbying, ownership) and a weight. Then the work was attacked:

  1. Hypercritical audit (E008) — every node, edge, and ranking checked against the evidence files; 12 wrong edges, 12 filler edges, and 45 missing entities found and fixed.
  2. Red-team review (E009) — every claim assumed wrong until a primary source (.gov, WA PDC, IRS 990s, Fitch, court records) confirmed it; ~80% survived, and every mischaracterized figure was corrected.
  3. Three skeptic subagents (E010) — ranked the ranking, the omissions, and the money separately, catching the Council over-rank, Kelly Chambers’ inflated score, the buried Tacoma mayor, and the wrong Chamber budget ($4.5M → $3.08M).
  4. Deep profiles (E013–E014) — all 163 entities researched against primary sources, 43 new edges, deterministic consistency sync (272 → 342 edge count), with per-entity confidence ratings.

Composite scores blend six domains; the interactive graph adds a veto-power bonus for actors whose influence is concentrated and non-negotiable (the Tribe, JBLM, the Sheriff, the Chamber) rather than distributed. Full method: evidence/26-power-methodology.md.
