## 0. Why this person or group is on the map - **Inclusion rule — power and decision-making.** Central Pierce Fire & Rescue is a special-purpose fire district. It is governed by an elected Board of Fire Commissioners under Title 52 of the Revised Code of Washington (RCW), the state law that covers fire districts. - It levies taxes on property owners in its service area. It controls a budget of more than $100 million. It runs fire and emergency medical services. - On January 1, 2026, the district merged with Graham Fire & Rescue and Orting Valley Fire & Rescue. That made it one of the largest fire districts in Pierce County. - **Adjacent but excluded:** East Pierce Fire & Rescue and West Pierce Fire & Rescue are peer districts mapped separately. South Pierce Fire & Rescue is adjacent and mapped separately. Pierce County Fire Training Consortium is a shared service entity and not a power node.

## 1. Who they are - **Legal name:** Central Pierce Fire & Rescue. Also known as Pierce County Fire District 6. - **Entity type:** Government — a special-purpose fire protection district under Title 52 RCW. Set up as a municipal corporation with taxing authority. - **Governing law:** Title 52 RCW. The Board of Fire Commissioners is "the legislative body of Central Pierce Fire & Rescue." - **Merger history:** On April 22, 2025, voters approved merging Graham Fire & Rescue and Orting Valley Fire & Rescue into Central Pierce. The Graham vote was 7,483 yes to 1,130 no. The merger took effect January 1, 2026. The three legacy districts are now one. - **Board:** After the merger, the board has 14 commissioners from the three legacy districts. Commissioners are elected at-large by voters. They serve six-year terms. - **Executive leadership:** Fire Chief Dustin Morrow.

## 3. Where their power comes from - **Statutory authority.** Under RCW 52, Central Pierce can levy voter-approved property taxes (Regular Fire Levy, EMS Levy), charge a Fire Benefit Charge (FBC), issue general obligation bonds with voter approval, and provide fire and emergency medical services within its borders. - **Taxing authority:** Three voter-approved funding sources: - Regular Fire Levy — about 40% of the budget. Rate was $0.50 per $1,000 of assessed value (AV) in each legacy district. A levy lid lift from $0.73 to $1.00 per $1,000 AV went on the April 2024 ballot (Resolution 24-01). A "levy lid lift" is a vote to raise the property-tax rate above the state cap. - EMS Levy — about 16% of the budget. 20.1% of Central Pierce funding in 2024. - Fire Benefit Charge (FBC) — about 36% of the budget. Based on property use and square footage, not assessed value. Must be reauthorized by voters every six years. - **Budget control:** The 2021 appropriated budget totaled $105,386,885 across six funds. General Fund: $64.9 million. EMS Fund: $16.6 million. GEMT (Ground Emergency Medical Transport) Fund: $12.2 million. Debt Fund: $2.5 million. Capital Projects Fund: $9.08 million. HRA/Flex: $50,000. Actual 2021 spending was $88.4 million. Post-merger 2026 totals are not verified. - **Domain overlaps:** - **Government ↔ Economic:** Taxing authority over property in the district; contracts for equipment and services. - **Government ↔ Institutional:** Interlocal agreements (formal cooperation contracts between governments) with peer fire districts; member of Pierce County Fire Training Consortium.

## 1. Who they are - **Verdict:** Central Pierce Fire & Rescue's power is derived from its statutory authority as a fire protection district under RCW Title 52, its voter-approved taxing powers (fire levy, EMS levy, fire benefit charge), and its control over emergency service delivery within its boundaries. The 2025–2026 merger with Graham and Orting Valley Fire & Rescue significantly expanded its territorial and fiscal footprint, making it one of the largest fire districts in Pierce County. Its Board of 14 elected commissioners exercises budgetary and operational governance. Its budget exceeded $105M (2021 appropriated) and has likely grown post-merger. - **Conflicts-of-interest flags:** The fire benefit charge (FBC) is calculated based on property use and square footage, creating a potential tension between the district's revenue needs and property owners' tax burden. The merger was promoted by district leadership and approved by voters — no evidence of improper influence found, but the consolidation reduces competitive service boundaries. Commissioner races are partisan-adjacent elections with low voter engagement, which could allow incumbent entrenchment. - **Analysis of alternatives :** An opposing reading would note that fire districts, while possessing taxing authority, are functionally service-delivery entities with limited political power beyond their boundaries. Their budgets are constrained by voter approval requirements (levy lid lifts, FBC reauthorization, bond votes). The merger, while expanding the district, also creates governance challenges — 14 commissioners from three legacy districts may create internal friction. The district's power is primarily fiscal and operational rather than political. - **Confidence:** Medium — based on strong primary sources (district website, SAO audit, official resolutions) for structural and financial facts, but post-merger 2026 budget totals and specific commissioner PDC campaign finance data not fully extracted in this pass.
