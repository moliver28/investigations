# How the World Works: Structural Gaps in the Lakewood Story
**Date:** 2026-08-16  
**Purpose:** Not evidence gaps — understanding gaps. What a reader needs to know about how power actually works to understand WHY this situation exists, not just WHAT happened.

---

## The Questions a Reader Can't Answer From Our Evidence Alone

A reader who sees "56 meetings, 16 years, 1 recusal, 5 of 7 council members employed by other governments or public agencies" will ask: **how is this legal?** Or: **why would anyone expect something different?** Those are structural questions. Here's what we need to understand to answer them.

---

## Gap 1: The Part-Time Council Paradox

**The structural reality:** Lakewood city council members are paid approximately **$300-470 per meeting** (typical for WA code cities of this size). The position is explicitly designed as part-time. For a city of ~63,000 people (WA's 21st largest), the council meets ~2-4 times per month.

**The consequence:** The system **requires** members to have outside employment. No one can serve on a part-time council with full-time responsibilities without a day job. But every day job creates potential conflicts with city business. A teacher whose school district contracts with the city. A small business owner whose competitors need permits. A county employee whose employer overlaps with city services.

**The question this raises:** Are these 7 people unusually conflicted? Or is it structurally impossible to run a part-time city council without members having conflicts of interest?

**The truth:** It's the latter. The question is not whether conflicts exist — they're baked into the design. The question is whether the system provides tools to manage them. Lakewood's system does not.

**Key data point:** Every single one of the 7 members has conflicts. That's not coincidence — it's the inevitable result of drawing 7 people from a community to serve part-time while maintaining careers.

---

## Gap 2: The Enforcement Vacuum

**The structural reality:** Washington has three ethics/enforcement bodies — the Public Disclosure Commission (campaign finance), the Executive Ethics Board (state employees), and the Legislative Ethics Board (state legislators). **There is no state body that enforces RCW 42.23** (municipal conflicts of interest) against city council members.

Enforcement for municipal ethics violations in WA code cities is a **complete vacuum**:

| Possible Enforcer | Why They Can't / Won't |
|------------------|----------------------|
| City attorney | Works FOR the council. Cannot investigate or prosecute her employers. |
| City manager | Also works FOR the council. Can't enforce rules against his bosses. |
| County prosecutor | Has jurisdiction but no mandate. Typically under-resourced for white-collar municipal ethics. |
| WA PDC | Only handles campaign finance (RCW 42.17A) and F-1 disclosures. Not RCW 42.23. |
| WA State Auditor | Audits financial compliance, not ethics. No ethics mandate. |
| Citizen lawsuit | Possible but expensive ($10K+ in legal fees) and requires clear statutory violation. |
| State Attorney General | Can intervene but rarely does for local ethics matters. |
| Voters | Only recourse is at next election — and most council members run unopposed. |

**The consequence:** RCW 42.23 has been on the books since 1972. The PDC has enforced campaign finance rules for 50+ years. But municipal ethics violations — the actual conflicts of interest that affect daily governance — have **no dedicated enforcement body.**

**Lakewood is not unusual in this.** Most WA code cities (council-manager form) have the same enforcement vacuum. Seattle (SEEC) and Tacoma (Board of Ethics) are exceptions, not the rule.

## Gap 3: The City Manager Trap

**The structural reality:** Lakewood is a **council-manager** code city. The council sets policy; the city manager (Doug Russell, hired Aug 2025, $265K salary) runs operations.

**The trap:** The council is the city manager's **boss**. They hire, fire, and evaluate him. He reports to them. He cannot police their ethics because they control his employment.

Meanwhile, the city attorney — the person most qualified to advise on conflicts — also reports to the council. If the city attorney says "Mayor Bocchi, you have a conflict on this vote," Bocchi could theoretically respond "find me a new city attorney."

**The consequence:** In a council-manager system, there is **no one in the organizational chart** whose job includes holding the council accountable for ethics. The checks and balances that exist in state government (separate executive, legislative, judicial branches) don't exist at the municipal level.

---

## Gap 4: The "Common Law Incompatible Offices" Doctrine That Nobody Uses

**The structural reality:** Washington has a **common law incompatible offices** doctrine dating to **1909** (State ex rel. Robinson v. School District). It holds that one person cannot hold two positions if the duties are inconsistent, if one is subordinate to the other, or if there's a conflict of interest.

**The problem:** This doctrine has **never been applied** to municipal council members who also work for other governments. It's been law for 115+ years, and it's most commonly cited in the context of holding two elected offices simultaneously — not holding one elected office while being employed by a different government.

**Key question:** Does the incompatible offices doctrine prohibit someone from being a Pierce County employee AND a Lakewood city council member simultaneously? The answer is: **we don't know, because it's never been litigated.** The doctrine exists, it would seem to apply, but no WA court has ruled on this specific question.

**This matters because:** If the doctrine DOES apply, then Bocchi's 15-year dual role may be void ab initio (invalid from the start). If it DOESN'T apply, then the legislature would need to act to close the gap.

## Gap 5: The $2,400 Threshold & The Narrowing of "Beneficial Interest"

**The structural reality:** RCW 42.23.030 says no municipal officer shall be "beneficially interested" in a contract. But over decades, the definition of "beneficial interest" has been **narrowed by court decisions and legislative amendments:**

- In 2022, the legislature passed SB 5735, doubling the threshold for allowable beneficial interest to **$4,000** (from $2,000)
- "Remote interests" are exempt — including less than 1% corporate ownership
- The statute is limited to actual **contracts** — not to broader policy votes that create conflicts

**The consequence:** What the public thinks is illegal (voting on something that benefits you financially) may not actually violate the statute. RCW 42.23 doesn't say "don't vote on things that benefit you." It says "don't have a financial interest in a specific contract the city enters into." Those are very different things.

**For Lindholm specifically:** His F-1 may not show Concord Counsel income, but RCW 42.17's F-1 threshold is $2,400. If his business generated under $2,400 personally in 2025, he may have complied with the law. And his votes on development regulations are not the same as having a "beneficial interest in a contract" — the regulations are policy, not a specific contract.

**This is the gap between public expectations and legal reality.** Most readers think "conflict of interest" covers anything that benefits someone. The actual legal prohibition is significantly narrower.

## Gap 6: The Comparison Problem — Is Lakewood Broken or Normal?

**The structural reality:** We've been saying "Lakewood's ethics framework is broken." But we haven't established what "fixed" looks like or whether Lakewood is worse than peers.

**Key comparative questions with no evidence:**

| Question | What We'd Need to Answer | Current Status |
|----------|--------------------------|----------------|
| How many WA code cities have independent ethics boards? | Survey or MRSC data | **Unknown** — we know Seattle and Tacoma do, but they're charter cities, not code cities |
| What's the recusal rate in peer cities (population 50-70K)? | Meeting minutes from Lacey, Puyallup, Richland, Shoreline | **Not collected** |
| Do other WA councils have dual-government employees? | Employment data for council members in peer cities | **Not collected** |
| How do peer cities handle the part-time council paradox? | Training materials, ethics policies from comparable cities | **Not collected** |

**The honest answer:** We don't know if Lakewood is typical or an outlier, because we haven't done the comparison. The zero-recusal pattern could be universal across WA code cities. If it is, the story isn't "Lakewood is broken" — it's "WA's code city system produces this result everywhere."

**Recommendation:** Before publishing the "systemic failure" frame, we need to know whether Lakewood is an example of the problem or an exception to the rule.

## Gap 7: The Incentive Question — Why Would Anyone Recuse?

**The structural reality:** In Lakewood's system, there are **zero incentives to recuse** and **zero penalties for not recusing:**

| Factor | Recusal Incentive? | Non-Recusal Penalty? |
|--------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Legal | RCW 42.23 technically requires it | No enforcement mechanism exists |
| Political | Voters might notice? (They won't — no one covers recusals) | Voters won't notice — 30% turnout, unopposed races |
| Social | Colleagues might appreciate the integrity | Being the "ethics scold" makes meetings awkward |
| Personal | None — you lose a vote | You keep your vote and nothing happens |

**The rational choice for any council member:** Never recuse. The system provides no benefit for doing so and no penalty for not doing so.

**This is the most important structural insight.** The Lakewood story isn't about 7 people who individually failed to meet an ethical standard. It's about a system where the rational choice for every member is to never recuse — and 16 years of data confirms that every member has made the rational choice.

---

## Gap 8: Who Benefits From the Status Quo?

**The structural reality:** The people who benefit from a system with no ethics enforcement are:
1. **Council members themselves** — they can vote freely on matters affecting their interests
2. **The city manager** — a council with recusal issues means vacancies, tie votes, and complications
3. **The development community** — when a real estate broker votes on development policy without recusing, developers get a friendly voice
4. **Incumbents** — no enforcement means no accountability, which means easier re-election

**The people who lose:**
1. **Residents** — their council members have split loyalties
2. **Taxpayers** — decisions may favor private interests over public good
3. **Ethical council members (if any)** — they're at a disadvantage relative to those who don't worry about conflicts

## The Story These Gaps Reveal

The investigation has proven what happened in Lakewood. These gaps reveal **why it happened** — and why it would happen in any WA code city with the same structure.

The real story isn't: *"Lakewood council members have conflicts and don't recuse."*

The real story is: ***"Washington's code city system produces councils where conflicts are inevitable, recusal is irrational, enforcement is nonexistent, and 16 years of data proves the system works exactly as designed."** *

## ✅ Gap 6 Resolved: Peer Comparison (Updated Aug 17)

**The question:** Is Lakewood broken, or is this standard for WA code cities?

**The answer (from 5 peer cities): BOTH.**

| Metric | Lakewood | Peer Average | Verdict |
|--------|----------|-------------|---------|
| Dual-government council members | **5 of 7 (71%)** | 0–29% (median 14%) | **CLEAR OUTLIER — ~2.4x the highest peer** |
| Recusals per 56 meetings | **1 (Brandstetter, 2010)** | Zero-range in peers | **MODERATE OUTLIER — extreme but not unique** |
| Independent ethics board | **NO** | 4 of 5 peers also NO | **ENTIRELY TYPICAL — WA code city standard** |

### The Three-Tier Verdict

**1. Dual-employment concentration IS exceptional.** 71% (5 of 7) vs a 0–29% peer range (median 14%) — recounted 2026-10-04 under a written rule; see `dual-government-recount.md`. This demands explanation.

**2. Recusal culture IS weak but not uniquely so.** Peers also show minimal recusal activity. The Richland finding is instructive: a councilmember TRIED to recuse (Jan 2026) and the mayor told her "abstentions are not allowed" — the same recusal-denial pattern Lakewood's system enables.

**3. Ethics enforcement gap IS the norm, not the exception.** 4 of 5 peer cities also lack independent ethics boards. Only Lynnwood (smaller, ~40K) has a functioning 5-member Board of Ethics with independent legal counsel. The absence of ethics enforcement is a WA-wide governance weakness.

**The real story:** Lakewood is worse than peers on the things that matter (dual-government concentration, recusal rate) and typical on the structural failure (no ethics enforcement). But the WA code city standard itself is broken — Lakewood is just the extreme case.

**Method:** 5 peer WA code cities (Puyallup, Lacey, Richland, Shoreline, Lynnwood) analyzed for recusal rate, dual-government employment, and ethics enforcement structure.
