## 0. Why this person or group is on the map - Tacoma Public Utilities (TPU) is the city’s electric, water, and rail company. It serves about 187,000 homes and businesses across central Pierce County, including Joint Base Lewis-McChord (JBLM), the big military base near Tacoma. - TPU is on this map for four reasons: it holds a public office (the city gave it the right to be the only electric and water provider in its area); it makes big decisions (its board sets rates and runs the city’s largest budget); it has a long record (130 years in operation); and it is well-connected (its CEO sits on national utility boards, and its rail arm moves freight for the Port of Tacoma). - **Adjacent but EXCLUDED candidates:** The 5-member Public Utility Board is not a separate node — it is covered below as the group that governs TPU. Tacoma City Council is a separate node. Puget Sound Energy (PSE) is the main private power company in the region and is mapped on its own. The state’s utility regulator (the Washington Utilities and Transportation Commission, or UTC) is excluded — TPU is not under it. The Tacoma-Pierce County Economic Development Board (EDB) is a separate node. Click! Network was TPU’s cable and internet arm — UNVERIFIED whether it still operates under TPU after the 2024 sale.

## 1. Who they are - **Exact legal name:** Tacoma Public Utilities. It is a department of the City of Tacoma. Its three divisions are Tacoma Power, Tacoma Water, and Tacoma Rail. - **Entity type:** A department of city government, not a private company. Voters created it in 1893 when they bought out a private firm called Tacoma Light & Water Company. It does not aim to make a profit for owners. It runs on money from the bills customers pay, not from taxes. - **Tax ID (EIN)/registration:** It uses the City of Tacoma’s EIN. There is no separate EIN because it is a city department, not its own company. - **Parent/subsidiaries:** The parent is the City of Tacoma. The three operating divisions are Tacoma Power (electricity), Tacoma Water (drinking water), and Tacoma Rail (a short freight railroad serving the Port of Tacoma, Lakeview, and the Frederickson industrial area). - **Ownership chain:** Public. The City of Tacoma owns it. There are no private shareholders. Money left over goes back into the system. - **Governing law:** The Tacoma City Charter and Washington State laws on city utilities. The state regulator (UTC) does not oversee TPU because it is a city utility. - **Founded:** 1893, when voters bought the old private company. The first big dam (Mayfield Dam) came online in 1929. The rail division was added later. - **Headquarters:** Public Utilities Building, 3628 South 35th Street, Tacoma, WA 98409.

## 3. Where their power comes from - **Power held:** TPU has the power of a city-owned monopoly. In its service area, it is the only legal electric, water, and rail provider. It is the largest department in Tacoma’s city government. Its power comes from: the city charter (the law that gives it the franchise); market share (about 187,000 customers over a 180 square-mile electric service area); self-rule (it answers to the City Council, not the state regulator); its wires and dams (2,286 miles of power lines plus a large hydro dam system); and freight rail (it runs the trains in and out of the Port of Tacoma, a key shipping hub). - **Government domain overlap:** TPU is a government body that also acts like a big business. It collects its own money (about $1.2 billion a year in the 2025–2026 budget). Its board and the City Council approve its budget. It spends money on pipes, wires, and rails across Pierce County. - **Budget:** About $1.2 billion a year for 2025–2026 (a two-year budget). Tacoma Power alone reported $516.5 million in operating revenue in 2024. - **Jobs controlled:** Between 1,001 and 5,000 workers (the LinkedIn range). Tacoma Power alone has about 805 workers. Citywide, the utility had about 1,777 workers in 2018. - **Rate authority:** The 5-member Public Utility Board recommends rates and the budget. The Tacoma City Council gives final approval. For 2025–2026, the Council approved a 6.5% electric rate hike and a 6.3% water rate hike. - **Land/real-estate:** TPU owns a set of old dams (Mayfield, Cushman, and others), 2,286 miles of power lines (1,524 above ground, 862 below), and rail track in and around the Port of Tacoma and Frederickson.

## 1. Who they are - **Verdict:** Tacoma Public Utilities runs the city’s electric, water, and rail service for about 187,000 customers across central Pierce County. It has a $1.2 billion two-year budget and between 1,001 and 5,000 workers. Its power comes from being the only legal provider in its area (a city monopoly since 1893) and from owning 2,286 miles of power lines, a set of dams, and the freight railroad at the Port. It answers to its own Public Utility Board and the Tacoma City Council — not the state regulator — which gives it more freedom to set rates than private utilities like PSE have. - **Conflicts-of-interest flags:** TPU’s not-for-profit setup shields it from some abuses private utilities face, but it also answers to no outside rate regulator. CEO Jackie Flowers sits on several outside boards (Large Public Power Council, APPA, Grid Forward, the Chamber, the EDB). She runs TPU and also sits on regional boards that push for the same industrial customers TPU serves — a possible conflict. The defense: utility CEOs often hold such seats to represent their city in national policy. The Public Utility Board’s 5-year appointed terms (not elected) keep rate decisions away from voters. That is good for expertise, but limits public say. The 2025–2026 6.5% rate hike passed without a fight, showing the current setup usually goes TPU’s way. - **Analysis of alternatives:** The opposing view is that TPU is held in check by the City Council’s budget approval, by public-notice rules, and by customers’ option to move (limited — there is no other electric provider in the area). Under this reading, TPU is a well-run public utility with proper checks. But the 2024–2025 rate cycle shows a different picture: TPU’s board approved the rate hike on October 23, 2024, and the City Council approved the budget in November 2024. Both votes were largely pro-forma, with no real changes. The real story is that TPU has far more freedom to set rates than its private peer PSE has before the UTC. - **Confidence:** Medium-High.
